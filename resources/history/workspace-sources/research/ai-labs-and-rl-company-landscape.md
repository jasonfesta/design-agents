# Emerging AI Labs and Reinforcement Learning Company Landscape

_Research snapshot: August 18, 2026_

## Scope

This document consolidates three company directories into a single market map:

1. [CleverHack’s Neolab and Emerging AI Lab Tracker](https://cleverhack.com/neolab-and-emerging-ai-lab-tracker), a broad directory of new AI research labs.
2. [RL List](https://www.rl-list.com/), a directory of 38 reinforcement-learning (RL) environment vendors and adjacent infrastructure providers, last updated July 15, 2026.
3. [Y Combinator’s Reinforcement Learning company directory](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/industry/Reinforcement%20Learning), an August 2026 list of YC companies tagged for RL.

The lists describe different layers of the ecosystem. “Neolab” describes an organizational model; “RL environment vendor” describes a product category; and YC’s “reinforcement learning” label includes vendors, infrastructure, applied-AI products, robotics, energy, and gaming. Inclusion is therefore not evidence that two companies compete directly.

## Executive takeaways

- **RL environments are becoming a distinct supplier category.** These companies build simulated software, tasks, reward functions, verifiers, trajectories, and evaluations used to train agents on realistic work.
- **The stack is separating into layers:** foundation/research labs; environment and data vendors; execution infrastructure; and end-user applications.
- **Coding, browser/computer use, and enterprise workflows dominate near-term environment supply.** They offer measurable outcomes and can often be reset and verified automatically.
- **Science and recursive improvement dominate the neolab narrative.** Many newer labs are pursuing automated research, world models, mathematical reasoning, physical intelligence, or self-improving systems.
- **San Francisco is the center of gravity, but not the whole market.** Meaningful clusters appear in New York, Boston/Cambridge, London, Paris, Germany, Canada, India, Switzerland, Singapore, and the UAE.
- **Directory labels require diligence.** Company-reported funding, customers, headcount, certifications, and research ties can be incomplete or self-claimed. Acquisitions and pivots also make point-in-time lists age quickly.

## Next research phase: downstream model builders

_Expanded in [Harvey Tenet Lookalikes: Vertical AI Companies That Own Their Models](./harvey-tenet-vertical-model-lookalikes.md)._

The next phase should focus on **vertical AI companies that consume foundation models, expert data, post-training systems, RL environments, and evaluation infrastructure to create proprietary domain models or agent systems**. Harvey is the archetype: it has publicly described custom legal-model work with OpenAI, domain-specific legal models, a multi-model strategy, proprietary evaluations, and post-training work with Applied Compute. “Own model” should not be limited to training a foundation model from scratch; it can include fine-tuned or post-trained weights, domain reward models, proprietary evals, model routing, and agent systems built on third-party base models.

### Research question

Which application companies are turning purchased model/data/environment infrastructure into differentiated domain intelligence—and which upstream vendors enable them?

### Proposed research plan

1. **Define the inclusion test.** Include companies with public evidence of at least one of: custom training or post-training, proprietary domain models, RL/RLHF, expert-data programs, proprietary benchmarks/evals, or formal model-provider training partnerships.
2. **Build a candidate universe by vertical.** Start with legal, healthcare/life sciences, finance, cybersecurity, coding, industrial/robotics, customer support, insurance, and accounting/tax.
3. **Map the supply chain.** For every downstream company, identify base-model providers, RL/environment vendors, data partners, compute/hosting providers, benchmark partners, and domain experts.
4. **Separate evidence from inference.** Label relationships as confirmed partnership, disclosed supplier/subprocessor, technical integration, hiring-derived signal, or unverified inference.
5. **Profile the model strategy.** Record whether differentiation lives in weights, data, evals, retrieval, tools/workflows, routing, or product distribution.
6. **Score candidates.** Rank on strength of proprietary-model evidence, depth of domain data, feedback-loop quality, measurable evaluation advantage, and defensibility.
7. **Produce two outputs.** Add a master company table to this report and create short profiles for the strongest 15–25 downstream model builders.

### Harvey as the reference profile

- **Downstream product:** professional AI for legal, tax, and finance work.
- **Model strategy:** custom-trained legal model work with OpenAI; domain-specific models; selection across multiple foundation-model providers; proprietary legal evaluations.
- **Recent technical evidence:** Harvey says it collaborated with Applied Compute to post-train GLM-5.1 for its Legal Agent Benchmark.
- **Important boundary:** Harvey states customer inputs and uploaded documents are not used to train underlying models. Research must distinguish proprietary/licensed training corpora and expert-created data from customer production data.
- **Upstream relationship to investigate:** identify which specialist data, environment, evaluation, and post-training vendors—if any—are publicly confirmed beyond OpenAI and Applied Compute.

## Diligence framework

For each company under serious consideration, validate:

1. **Company status:** independent, acquired, nonprofit, university-affiliated, stealth, or active commercial vendor.
2. **Actual deliverable:** environments, data, evals, training infrastructure, or a complete application.
3. **Environment quality:** fidelity, reset determinism, task diversity, long-horizon support, and resistance to contamination.
4. **Verifier quality:** objective success checks, partial-credit logic, side-effect detection, and reward-hacking controls.
5. **Evidence:** public benchmarks, reproducible research, named customers, work samples, and independently verifiable deployments.
6. **Security:** SOC 2 scope, data isolation, deployment model, retention, access controls, and support for private infrastructure.
7. **Commercial model:** pricing unit, exclusivity, ownership of generated trajectories, licensing, and portability.
8. **Operational capacity:** expert network, engineering headcount, throughput, turnaround time, and support.

## Source notes and limitations

- This is a **point-in-time synthesis**, not an independently audited database.
- Company descriptions are paraphrased from the three directories and should be checked against primary company materials before investment, procurement, or employment decisions.
- RL List explicitly notes that public figures can become stale quickly and that some claims are vendor-supplied. It recommends comparing work samples from a shortlist rather than selecting from rankings alone.
- YC company tags are broad and mutable. A company’s presence on the RL page does not establish that RL is its primary product or that it sells RL infrastructure.
- CleverHack’s tracker includes labs that are not conventional companies. These are labeled where material.
- Funding, valuation, headcount, customer, and security claims were intentionally omitted from most tables unless status context required them; those fields have the highest volatility and should be reverified directly.
